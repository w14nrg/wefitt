Fencing and gates in Fulham

A fence has to stand up to wind for years. That comes down to the posts: how deep they go, what they are set in and what they are made of. We build fences and gates that stay straight.

What we fit

  • Horizontal slatted fencing. The modern London garden look, with gaps for light and air or tight for privacy.
  • Closeboard and featheredge. Solid, strong and private, built on site board by board.
  • Panel fencing. Quicker and cheaper, best between concrete posts with gravel boards.
  • Gates. Side gates with bolts and locks, garden gates and front gates matched to the fence.

What's involved, step by step

1. Check the boundary

Who owns the fence, and where is the line? We check before taking anything down.

2. Set the posts

Posts go in deep, roughly a quarter of their length below ground, set in concrete or postcrete, plumb and in line.

3. Gravel boards

A board along the bottom keeps the fence timber off the soil, so it doesn't rot from the ground up.

4. Rails and boards

Rails fixed, then boards or slats spaced evenly with stainless or galvanised fixings that won't stain.

5. Gates

Hung on strong posts with heavy hinges, with a drop bolt, latch and lock where needed, and checked to swing clear.

Planning a new fence? Check these first

  • Agree the work with your neighbour if the fence is shared, and check who owns it.
  • Fences up to 2 m high are usually allowed without planning permission, or 1 m next to a road or footpath.
  • Listed buildings and some conservation areas have extra rules on front boundaries.
  • Think about the gate width you need for bins and bikes.

How long does it take?

A typical back garden fence is usually two to three days, depending on how many posts and how hard the ground is.

Rules and regulations

Under the householder permitted development rules, fences, walls and gates are generally allowed up to 2 m high, or 1 m where they are next to a road or footpath used by vehicles. Listed buildings, some conservation areas and flats can be different.

Questions people ask

Concrete or timber posts?

Concrete posts last longer and won't rot. Timber posts look better with slatted designs. We often use timber posts set on or in concrete for slatted fences.

Can you replace just a few panels?

Yes, if the posts are sound. Rotten posts mean the run needs rebuilding.

Can you make the side gate more secure?

Yes, with stronger hinges, a lock, a drop bolt and sometimes a taller gate.
