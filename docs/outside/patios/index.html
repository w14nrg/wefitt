What we fit

  • Porcelain paving. Very hard-wearing and stain-resistant, laid with a special primer as it doesn't absorb mortar.
  • Natural stone. Sandstone, limestone and granite for a softer, traditional look.
  • Front paths. Victorian-style tiled or stone paths to front doors.
  • Steps and edging. Steps up to the garden and neat edges to beds and lawns.

What's involved, step by step

1. Dig out

Down far enough for the sub-base, bedding and slab, keeping the finished level at least 150 mm below the damp-proof course.

2. Sub-base

A compacted layer of crushed stone, typically around 100 mm, so the patio doesn't sink.

3. Full mortar bed

Every slab laid on a full bed of mortar, not five dots, so there are no hollows to crack or rock.

4. Falls

A gentle slope, roughly 1 in 60 to 1 in 80, so water runs away from the house to a drain or a bed.

5. Joints

Pointed with mortar or a modern jointing compound, then cleaned off.

Choosing paving? Check these first

  • Porcelain needs a bonding primer on the back. Without it, slabs can come loose.
  • Order 10% extra for cuts.
  • Paving more than 5 m² of a front garden with a hard surface that doesn't drain needs planning permission.
  • Some sandstones mark easily. Ask about sealing.

How long does it take?

A typical small back garden patio usually takes three to five days, including digging out and the sub-base.

Rules and regulations

Since 2008, paving more than 5 m² of a front garden needs planning permission unless the surface is permeable or the water drains to a lawn or border. Back gardens are usually fine.

Questions people ask

Why do patios go green?

Shade and standing water. Good falls and the right joint material help, and porcelain is easiest to clean.

Can you lay a patio over the old one?

Rarely a good idea. Raising the level can push it above the damp-proof course, and the old base may be failing.

Can you lay a front path in Victorian tiles?

Yes, geometric tiled paths are a popular choice for Fulham and Chelsea front gardens.
